A data story

The risk didn't grow.
It changed hands.

Every country depends on suppliers it could lose overnight. This story traces that vulnerability across 87 economies and thirty years of real trade data, then follows one country closely, Türkiye, in twelve short scenes: first how the world moved, then how Türkiye's own exposure quietly changed hands, as both buyer and seller.

Scene 1 / 12

What even is this number?

The usual question is "how much do you import from a country?" The better question is "could you replace that supplier tomorrow?" Two countries can buy the exact same share of their steel from abroad, one from five suppliers, one from a single supplier, and face wildly different risk. It isn't hypothetical: 34.7% of Türkiye's own import value already comes from products where a single supplier holds half the market or more. This index scores that difference. Try it, using the buttons above the chart.

The formula multiplies each sector's share of a country's imports by how concentrated its suppliers are (a Herfindahl index, where 1.0 means a single supplier), then discounts that by how easily buyers can substitute between supplier countries for that good, an elasticity estimated per sector by Broda, Greenfield and Weinstein. Because the math is additive, a claim like "China is 33% of Türkiye's import risk" isn't an estimate, it's an exact accounting identity that sums to the total. Figures throughout use the "core" basis, excluding gold, which is volatile and swamps the index in years of heavy bullion flows.

Scene 2 / 12

This isn't just a Türkiye story

Across 87 economies, the typical country's import concentration fell for over a decade after 1995, the high tide of globalization, then swung back. By 2024 the world's median country was about as concentrated as it was in 1995. Whatever happens to Türkiye in this story, it's happening almost everywhere.

The median country's import concentration fell from 49 to 45 during the hyper-globalization years of 1995 to 2008, then climbed back to 49 by 2024, exactly where it started. Some countries diversified hard against that current, Mexico's concentration fell 81 points, Canada's 52, while Russia rose 59 points and China-facing Asia re-concentrated alongside it. Türkiye's own 30-year net move: zero, a flat aggregate hiding the same rotation playing out underneath.

Scene 3 / 12

Two shadows, not one

Among the world's major economies, China is the single biggest source of import risk for 37 of them. The United States is the biggest source for just 8, Germany for 10. China's shadow reaches every continent; America's mostly stays in its own hemisphere. Click a name above the chart to see who's standing in whose shadow.

One exception in the US group: Ireland, likely pharmaceutical imports rather than geography.

Widen the lens and 14 major economies now draw at least half their import risk from China alone: Russia (86%), Hong Kong (78%) and Vietnam (72%) among them; India gets there on just 20% of its import value. Even the US's own import risk is 34% China, Germany's is 27%, Türkiye's is 33%. That's because China's risk share consistently runs 1.5 to 2.5 times its plain import share, it dominates precisely the sectors that are hardest to switch away from, not just the biggest ones.

Scene 4 / 12

What happens when you weaponize trade

For 27 years Russia's own import concentration barely moved. Then came the 2022 sanctions: cut off from Western suppliers, its imports rushed toward the one major power still willing to sell to it. In two years the index jumped from the high-50s to over 90, a live demonstration of what happens when a country's supplier options disappear overnight.

Before 2022, Russia's own import-concentration score sat around 61, unremarkable for a major economy. Within two years of the sanctions regime it hit 91, the highest of any major economy in the panel, as its supplier base compressed into near-total dependence on China. It is the clearest natural experiment in the whole dataset: cut a country off from most of its suppliers, and its exposure to whichever supplier remains spikes almost immediately.

Scene 5 / 12

Now, Türkiye's own line

For most of three decades, Türkiye's import concentration held in a narrow, unremarkable band. Then, starting in 2020, it began climbing, reaching its highest point since the early 2000s by 2024. The next four scenes are about why.

On the core (ex-gold) basis, Türkiye's import index measured 27.6 in 1995, drifted down to a low of 23.6 in 2019, then climbed for five straight years to 27.4 by 2024, its highest reading since the early 2000s. The 25 years in between weren't static underneath, either, the aggregate held in a narrow band while, scene by scene, almost every supplier behind it changed.

Scene 6 / 12

The rotation

The line barely moved. Who's behind it changed completely. In 1995, Germany and the EU dominated Türkiye's imports; the US mattered too. By 2024, China's share had grown many times over, and Russia's held firm through a war. Click a name in the chart to trace just that one line.

Toggle to exports above the chart for the mirror rotation: Germany's dominance as a buyer fading as Türkiye found others, Iraq emerging almost from zero.

In 1995, China supplied just 1.8% of Türkiye's import risk. It passed the US in 2004, Germany in 2006, and the entire EU-27 bloc combined in 2013, reaching 34.6% by 2024. Over the same period, the EU and US together fell from 62% of Türkiye's import risk to 24%; China and Russia alone now carry 54% between them. The headline number barely moved because one dependence was quietly swapped for another.

One caveat worth flagging: Türkiye's recorded energy trade with Russia is probably understated. Turkish statistical confidentiality rules, combined with Russia's own non-reporting after 2022, mean pipeline-gas and oil partner attribution in the underlying trade data is indicative rather than exact. If anything, that makes Russia's true share of this rotation a lower bound, not an overstatement.

Scene 7 / 12

Why the small numbers lie

Drag the slider below the chart to trace twelve of Türkiye's major partners' paths since 1995, colored by region. China's dot has walked steadily up and to the right: 2% of Türkiye's imports and barely more risk in 1995, now 15% of imports but 35% of the risk, more than double its weight. Germany's dot has drifted the other way, from 1.5× its import share in 1995 to under 1× today, a similar-sized supplier that has grown steadily more replaceable. Size and risk are not the same thing, and which countries carry the gap between them keeps changing.

Toggle to exports for Türkiye's top buyers instead of suppliers: Germany still leads but at a shrinking multiplier, and Iraq's dot doesn't exist before 2003.

China and Russia each contribute roughly twice their import weight to Türkiye's risk total; Brazil contributes about three times its weight. Japan sits at the opposite extreme: 1.5% of imports, but only 0.3% of the risk, because Japanese goods are easy to source elsewhere. The Russia and China cases aren't the same kind of dependence, though. Russia dominates products where the world market is genuinely deep (it's just 5% of world oil-product exports, 11% of wheat), so switching is expensive but possible. Where China dominates, it's often also the world's largest exporter for that product, 59% of the value in the sectors it leads, so there's frequently nowhere else at scale to buy from.

Scene 8 / 12

The chokepoints you've never heard of

These are the products, not the countries, where a broken relationship would actually hurt: Türkiye imports of a single item, worth real money, from one dominant supplier. Drag the slider below the chart to see the top 10 by value, for any year back to 1995. Fossil fuels almost always take several of the top slots, but which country supplies them keeps changing, Russia, Algeria and Azerbaijan have each held the top natural-gas slot at different points. Aircraft, cars, steel and electronics fill out the rest, one dominant seller at a time, rarely the same one for long.

Concentration measured at the broad sector level badly understates the real picture: the value-weighted supplier HHI is 0.14 at that resolution but 0.29 once you look at the finer, product level, and 34.7% of Türkiye's import value comes from products where a single supplier already holds half the market or more. The most extreme cases are cheap by value but near-total by supplier share: vanadium oxides (100% Russia), magnesium (95% China), phosphate rock (88% Morocco) and neodymium magnets (85% China) together account for only 0.32% of the import bill.

Scene 9 / 12

What Ankara holds

Dependence runs both ways. Türkiye is itself the dominant world supplier of dozens of products: dried apricots, hazelnuts, refined borax, machine-woven carpets. Drag the slider below the chart to see the top 10 for any year back to 1995, the same handful of Anatolian commodities keep reappearing, though the buyer often changes (China only became the biggest buyer of Turkish marble after 2010). Here, Türkiye holds the leverage, and other countries are the ones exposed.

Across 36 products with world markets worth over $200 million each, Türkiye supplies at least a fifth of global exports, and for several it's the outright dominant seller: refined borax (64% of world exports), dried apricots (74%), hazelnuts (62%), machine-woven carpets (66%) and, more recently, drones (around 65%). These are the mirror image of the chokepoints in the previous scene, sectors where Türkiye, not some foreign supplier, is the one other countries would struggle to replace.

Scene 10 / 12

The export mirror

Everything so far has been about buying. Selling tells the opposite story. In 1995, Türkiye's exports were an apparel monoculture sold almost entirely to one buyer, Germany alone carried 56% of all export risk. Thirty years on, that risk has fallen 58%, from 159 to 67. The EU is still the anchor, but at half its old weight, and no single non-EU buyer tops 11%.

The 58% drop is the single largest move of any series in the dataset, and it came with a technology shift, not just a diversification one: the low-tech export risk share (mostly apparel) fell from 54.7% to 22.8%, while medium-tech exports (machinery, vehicles, parts) grew from 14.4% to 33.9% of export risk. Türkiye's exports climbed the technology ladder over these three decades; its imports, by contrast, are structured almost the same today as they were in 1995.

Scene 11 / 12

Most of Türkiye's concentration is a choice

Compare Türkiye's supplier concentration in each sector to the same sector in nine peer economies. Where Türkiye sits above its peers but the sector itself is diversified worldwide, that's a procurement choice, not a market fact, 22% of import value falls in that fixable zone, led by a single sector: refined petroleum, $27.6bn. Only 8% is concentrated for everyone, aircraft, wheat, displays, a function of how the global market is built.

There's a related asymmetry worth noting: Türkiye's single biggest import line, machinery and vehicles at 40% of the import bill, is actually one of its least risky, contributing only 27% of total risk, because nearly every country sells them. Across the board, medium-tech goods carry less risk than their size would suggest (an amplification of 0.67, meaning risk share runs well below import share), while every other tier, high-tech, resource-based, low-tech and primary goods, runs at or above its import weight. The pattern holds across dozens of major economies, not just Türkiye.

Scene 12 / 12

Who's really EU-locked?

Treat the EU-27 as a single supplier, one customs union, one sanctions policy, and Türkiye's measured concentration roughly doubles, +24. That sounds alarming until you see the same test applied to Türkiye's neighbors: Switzerland jumps +77, Norway +62, North Macedonia +58, the UK +52, Morocco +45, Tunisia +43. Against its own neighborhood, Türkiye is one of the least EU-locked economies around.

All of these are economies with deep, geographically-anchored trade ties to the EU, whether or not they're members: Switzerland and Norway trade with the EU under bilateral and single-market arrangements, North Macedonia and the Western Balkans are accession-track candidates, and the UK remains heavily EU-integrated despite Brexit. Türkiye's own EU customs union, in place since 1995 to 1996, is exactly this kind of tie, which is why treating the bloc as one supplier moves its number at all. The point isn't that Türkiye is unexposed to the EU, it's that its neighbors, by this same test, are typically more so.
